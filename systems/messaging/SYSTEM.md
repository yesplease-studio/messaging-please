# Messaging System

A methodology for building messaging architecture that connects purpose and positioning to what a company says to each specific person in a deal. Treats messaging as a chain: purpose anchors positioning, positioning anchors the core claim, the core claim translates per audience, the translation feeds the GTM narrative. Humans provide judgment and approval. AI handles structure, synthesis, and artifact production.

---

## 1. Core Concepts

### 1.1 The Messaging Chain

```
Purpose
  ↓
Positioning
  ↓
Core Claim
  ↓
Per-Audience Translation
  ↓
GTM Narrative
```

This chain is the organizing principle of the entire system. Each layer depends on the one above it.

- If purpose is generic, positioning will be generic.
- If positioning is weak, the core claim has nothing to stake out.
- If the core claim is unclear, per-audience translation produces noise.
- If per-audience translation isn't done, the GTM narrative is just the core claim in a deck.

When a company says "we have a messaging problem," the real question is: where in the chain did it break? Most companies diagnose the symptom (inconsistent content, low-converting outbound, confused prospects) and try to fix at the GTM layer -- without realizing the break is at positioning or purpose. This system traces upward.

### 1.2 Why this matters financially

Purpose-driven brands are not just more meaningful -- they're more valuable. Jim Stengel's decade-long study of 50,000 brands identified a company's "ideal" (its purpose, the higher-order benefit it provides to people's lives) as the single strongest predictor of financial performance. His Stengel 50 -- the brands with the highest clarity of purpose -- outperformed the S&P 500 by 400% over 10 years.

The mechanism is not sentiment. It is clarity. A company with clear purpose makes faster decisions, attracts more aligned customers, builds higher loyalty, and communicates more coherently -- because everything routes back to the same anchor. Diluted purpose produces diluted messaging, which produces diluted conversion and churn.

This is why messaging-please starts at purpose. Not because it's nice to have a mission statement. Because purpose-clarity is a compounding financial asset.

**What to cite in teach-through-choice moments:**
- Jim Stengel / Millward Brown BrandZ: purpose-driven brands grow 3x faster than the market
- Deloitte Global Millennial Survey: purpose-driven companies report higher workforce satisfaction and retention
- Harvard Business Review (2021): companies that connect employees to purpose outperform peers on multiple financial metrics
- Gartner: the average B2B buying group has 6-10 decision-makers involved -- this is why per-audience translation exists

### 1.3 What the system does

The messaging system:

1. **Locks in context first.** Company profile, sibling artifacts, stakeholder structure, and competitive landscape before a single line of messaging is written.
2. **Establishes the foundation.** Purpose, mission, vision, and positioning before moving to audiences. The foundation is the anchor for everything else.
3. **Architects per-audience.** Builds a distinct message for each stakeholder in a deal, all rooted in the same core claim.
4. **Bridges to operations.** Connects the message architecture to the sales motion, product narrative, and pricing structure -- with explicit handoffs to the tools that own those domains.
5. **Maintains over time.** Surfaces drift and proposes targeted updates as the company evolves.

## 2. The Five Layers

### 2.1 Purpose

**Definition:** Why the company exists beyond making money. The answer to Simon Sinek's "Why."

Purpose is not a goal. It is not a target market. It is not a feature. It is the reason the company would be missed if it disappeared -- articulated in terms of the benefit to the world, not the benefit to the company.

**Good purpose:** "To accelerate the world's transition to sustainable energy." (Tesla) -- clear direction, clear beneficiary, clear aspiration beyond product.

**Bad purpose:** "To build the best software for operations teams." -- this is a product description. It has no purpose in it.

**Why we ask:** Purpose becomes the root of the positioning claim. If the purpose is clear, positioning follows naturally. If the purpose is vague, positioning becomes a negotiation about features.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Why did the founder start this company? What was broken in the world?
- What would be lost if this company didn't exist?
- What does the company believe that most competitors don't?
- What is the company ultimately trying to change -- not about itself, but about the people or world it serves?

**Purpose has a ceiling, not just a floor.** The well-known failure is a purpose that's really a product description -- too low, a feature wearing a why ("to build the best software for operations teams"). The subtler failure is the opposite: a purpose that has collapsed into the *how*, the mission restated as if it were the reason. "To build the foundation the next generation of technology stands on" is not a product description, so it slips past the usual check, but it describes what the company does, not why that matters. Guard both ends.

**Three tests for whether a purpose is at the right level:**

1. **No-machinery test.** If the purpose contains a technology or product noun -- "AI," "platform," "foundation," "systems," "software," "data" -- it is almost certainly the how or the what in disguise. Strip the nouns and the real why is forced to show itself.
2. **The "so that what?" ladder.** Purpose sits one level above vision. Read the purpose immediately before the vision: it should answer why the vision is even worth wanting. If purpose and vision feel like the same thought, the purpose hasn't climbed high enough.
3. **The swap test.** Could you lift this exact line and drop it on the most similar company in the category? If yes, it isn't yours. The most ownable purpose grows out of the company's specific bet -- the thing it believes and does that the others don't.

**Angles before phrasings.** When a purpose is hard to land, the problem is usually not the wording -- it's that the underlying *motive* hasn't been chosen. Generate three to five candidate purposes from genuinely different motives (e.g. extending human reach, making solving abundant, leaving no knowledge wasted), each as a short labeled angle. Compare the motives first, choose the one that's true, and only then wordsmith. The decision is which motive is yours; the sentence is disposable.

**Print vs. argue.** The purpose a company prints and the belief it says out loud in the room don't have to be the same line. A printed purpose can be calm and broad; the belief that makes a listener lean in can be sharper and more contrarian. Both are legitimate -- don't force one to do the other's job.

**A right word can still be a borrowed one.** A word can be exactly right for a company and still be overused in its category right now (the way "abundance" saturates AI keynotes). That doesn't disqualify it, but the line has to feel earned rather than lifted from the slide everyone has already seen. Tie the word to the company's specific mechanism or bet, and it reads as theirs; wave it as a vibe, and it reads as borrowed.

### 2.2 Mission and Vision

**Mission:** What the company does in service of its purpose. Present-tense operational commitment. Not aspirational -- actionable. If you can't hold the company accountable to it today, it's a vision, not a mission.

**Vision:** Where the company is going. The future state it is working toward. Aspirational, time-bounded by implication (not "we will someday" -- "we are building toward X").

**The distinction matters:** Mission is accountability. Vision is direction. A company without a clear mission has no self-test. A company without a clear vision has no north star.

**The three are ordered by level.** Purpose (why) sits above mission (how), which sits above the offering (what). The textbook confusion is mission vs. vision, but the more damaging one in practice is purpose collapsing *down* into mission -- the company states its approach and calls it a reason. When purpose and mission start to sound alike, the purpose is the one that needs to climb (see §2.1, the ceiling failure).

**Common failure modes:**
- Mission and vision are the same sentence, split arbitrarily
- Mission is so broad it applies to any company in the category
- Vision is a product feature disguised as a future state
- Purpose is the mission restated -- the *how* dressed up as the *why*

### 2.3 Positioning

**Definition:** The specific place the company occupies in a specific buyer's mind, relative to their alternatives.

Positioning is not a tagline. It is not a brand promise. It is not a description of what the product does. It is a claim about a buyer's world -- where this company fits in it, why it fits better than the alternatives, and what the buyer has to believe for that to be true.

**The positioning statement structure** (adapted from April Dunford's *Obviously Awesome*):

```
For [target customer]
who [has this need / faces this situation],
[Company] is the [category]
that [delivers this outcome]
because [of this unique capability / approach].

Unlike [alternatives / status quo],
we [do this differently / better / in a way that matters to them].
```

This is a working document, not ad copy. It should be specific enough that a new team member could use it to decide what to say in a sales call.

**What makes positioning fail:**
- Describing what the product does instead of what the buyer gets
- Claiming differentiation that the buyer doesn't perceive as relevant
- Positioning against a competitor instead of positioning for a buyer
- Stopping at "we help X do Y" -- that's a value proposition, not a position

**Facilitation note:** This is where the work is hardest without someone in the room who can push back. "We help companies grow their revenue" is not positioning. Pushing from that to an actual claim requires someone willing to say "that's still not a claim -- what do you have that nobody else has, from the buyer's perspective?"

### 2.4 Audience Architecture

**Why B2B messaging is a multi-audience problem**

In a typical B2B deal, 6-10 people are involved in the buying decision (Gartner). Each brings different priorities, different risk tolerance, and a different definition of success. A message optimized for the economic buyer will often land wrong with the end user -- not because the message is wrong, but because it's written for the wrong person.

The solution is not to water down the message until it works for everyone. That produces a message that lands for nobody. The solution is architecture: one core claim, multiple translations, all rooted in the same purpose.

**Standard stakeholder roles:**

| Role | Primary concern | What they need from messaging |
|------|----------------|-------------------------------|
| **Economic buyer** | ROI, risk, budget justification | Clear value in financial terms; evidence this won't fail |
| **Champion** | Outcome, internal credibility | Confidence the product delivers; ammunition to sell it up |
| **End user** | Daily workflow, efficiency | This makes my job easier or better; I can actually use it |
| **Technical evaluator** | Integration, security, stability | This fits; it won't break things; I can support it |
| **End consumer** (B2B2C) | Personal outcome, experience | This works for me; I trust it; it's worth my time |

Not every deal includes all roles. Some have additional ones (legal, compliance, procurement). The skill asks which roles are present before building the architecture.

**What per-audience messaging includes:**

For each relevant audience:
1. **Core claim (translated):** The central message for this person, rooted in the same positioning
2. **Proof points:** Evidence that makes the claim credible for this person's priorities
3. **Primary objection:** The most likely resistance and how to address it
4. **Register notes:** Tone and vocabulary adjustments for this audience (feeds voice-please)

### 2.5 GTM Narrative

**Definition:** How the messaging chain connects to the company's commercial motion -- what the sales team says, how the product tells its own story, and how pricing is framed in terms of the value it represents.

The GTM narrative is not a pitch deck. It is the underlying structure that makes a pitch deck coherent. It answers:

- What is the sales story arc? (Problem → stakes → solution → proof → call to action)
- How does the product present itself at key moments? (Onboarding, feature announcements, error states)
- How is pricing framed in terms of the value delivered, not the cost incurred?
- What are the explicit handoffs to the sales team, product team, and voice system?

**Why this layer exists:** A company can have excellent messaging architecture and still fail to operationalize it, because nobody translated it into the formats the team actually uses. The GTM narrative is the bridge from artifact to action. Without it, messaging lives in a document and dies there.

### 3.3 The natural handoff to sales-please

When `messaging-bridge` is complete, explicitly name the next step: take the message architecture to sales-please.

The reason: message architecture tells you *what* to say. Sales qualification tells you *who* the deal is for, *whether* it's worth pursuing, and *how* to prioritize. These are complementary systems, not competitors. A company with strong messaging but no qualification discipline will spend resources pitching the wrong deals with the right story.

Surface this explicitly. Say what sales-please does, why it's the natural next step, and what artifact from messaging-please to bring into it.

## 4. Learning Layer

This system teaches through use. Every skill run includes:

- **Teach-through-choice questions:** Each key question includes a one-line rationale explaining why it matters. In standard and teach modes, this appears inline. In expert mode, it's available on request.
- **Shape-check responses:** When a user provides a purpose, positioning statement, or message draft, the skill assesses it before moving on. Not linting -- tutoring.
- **Companion document:** Generated alongside every artifact. Explains why the output is structured as it is, what to reuse, and how to run the same play next time.
- **Playbook accretion:** Each companion contributes an entry to `messaging/playbook.md`, building a cumulative record of what was learned and decided.

**Core teaching moments by phase:**

*messaging-discover:*
- Why purpose is not a mission statement (and what the difference costs)
- Why "we help X do Y" is a value proposition, not a position
- The financial case for purpose-driven positioning (teach when the user questions the depth of work)

*messaging-architect:*
- Why buyer ≠ user and what happens when you ignore the distinction
- Why different messages for different audiences is precision, not inconsistency
- How to write a core claim vs. a feature description

*messaging-bridge:*
- Why messaging without operationalization dies in a document
- Why the handoff to sales-please is a systems question, not a preference

## 5. Banned Phrases

These phrases signal that the messaging hasn't done its work yet. When they appear in a draft, flag them and push deeper.

| Phrase | Problem |
|--------|---------|
| "We help [X] do [Y]" | This is a value proposition, not a position. What claim does it stake out? |
| "We enable..." | Passive framing. What does the buyer actually get? |
| "We're passionate about..." | Passion is not positioning. What does the company believe that competitors don't? |
| "Best-in-class" / "world-class" | Unverifiable. What specifically makes it best, for whom? |
| "We believe in..." | Beliefs aren't claims. What does the company do because of that belief? |
| "Disrupting" / "revolutionizing" | Category jargon. What is actually different about the approach? |
| "Our mission is to make X easier" | Easier is a minimum bar, not a claim. Easier than what, for whom, and by how much? |
| "Ecosystem" | Usually a hedge. What does the company actually provide? |
| "Leverage" | Management jargon. Use plain language. |
| "Empowering" | Generic. What specific capability or outcome does the buyer gain? |

## 6. Quality Standards

A good messaging artifact:

- **Names the claim specifically.** Not "we help operations teams move faster" -- "the only [category] that [specific outcome] without [the tradeoff the buyer hates most]."
- **Is anchored to a specific buyer.** The ICP is named in the positioning statement and in each audience's message. Generic positioning serves nobody.
- **Has testable proof points.** Proof points that can't be verified are marketing promises. Proof points grounded in evidence (data, customer stories, mechanisms) are credibility.
- **Traces back to purpose.** If you cut the purpose-brief and the rest of the architecture still makes sense, the purpose wasn't doing work.
- **Includes register notes.** Messaging without voice direction is half-done. Every audience's entry in the architecture should note the tone and vocabulary adjustments relevant to that person.
